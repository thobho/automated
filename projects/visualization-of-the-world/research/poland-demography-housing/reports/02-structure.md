# 02 - Demographic structure, vital rates, migration and households, Poland 2000-2025

Latest year: **2025** (GUS BDL, values as of 31 December or for the calendar year [S1]). Place classes as in `../README.md`. Where a source does not give the class split, the level is stated in each paragraph; nothing is estimated for a missing level. Data files are in `data/processed/` (one per theme, each row with `source_id`).

## 1. Age and sex structure (national; urban/rural where stated)
- **Pyramids** for 2000, 2010, 2020 and 2025 are national only; BDL has no age-by-sex table for urban or rural areas [S8]. The 0-14 share fell from 19.1% in 2000 to 14.2% in 2025 and the 65+ share rose from 12.4% to 21.1% (derived from [S8]; GUS publishes 21.1% for 2025 [S9]). The 0-4 cohort shrank from 1.98 million in 2000 to 1.40 million in 2025 [S8], and women aged 70+ grew from 2.02 to 3.34 million [S8].
- **Median age** rose from 35.1 years on 1 January 2000 to 43.4 on 1 January 2025 [S19]; GUS gives 43.8 for 31 December 2025 [S11]. In 2025 the urban median (44.8) is above the rural one (42.3) [S11]; BDL has urban/rural medians only from 2016.
- **Old-age dependency** (65+ per 100 persons of working age) climbed from 18.6 in 2003 to 32.6 in 2025 on the GUS definition (working age women 15-59, men 15-64) [S9]; on the Eurostat 15-64 convention it is 17.8 in 2000 and 32.7 in 2025 [S19]. **Total dependency** fell until 2010 (GUS: 60.7 in 2002 to 55.2 in 2010, then 71.8 in 2025) [S9]; the Eurostat measure went from 47.0 (2000) via 40.6 (2010) to 55.7 (2025) [S19]. In 2025 total dependency is higher in towns (74.2) than in the countryside (68.4) (derived from [S10], available from 2012).
- The two definitions differ in level, not in direction; they must not be mixed in one chart without saying so.

## 2. Births, deaths, natural increase, fertility, life expectancy
- **Births** fell from 378 thousand (2000) to a low of 354 thousand (2002), rebounded to 413 thousand (2010) and 402 thousand (2017), then collapsed to 238 thousand in 2025 [S12]. **Deaths** rose from 368 thousand (2000) to 520 thousand in 2021 (COVID-19 year) and 406 thousand in 2025 [S12].
- **Natural increase** was +10 thousand in 2000, negative in 2002-2005, positive in 2006-2012 (peak +35 thousand in 2008), negative again from 2013, and -188 thousand in 2021 and -167 thousand in 2025 [S12]. The countryside was positive in every year to 2014 (+20 thousand in 2000), dipped to -0.9 thousand in 2015, was positive again in 2016-2018, negative from 2019 and at -56 thousand in 2025; the towns were already negative in 2000 (-9.9 thousand) and reached -111 thousand in 2025 [S12]. Per 1000 residents in 2025: towns -5.0, villages -3.7 [S12].
- **Total fertility rate**: 1.37 in 2000 (Eurostat [S21]), 1.25 in 2002, 1.38 in 2010, a peak of 1.45 in 2017 and 1.07 in 2025 (GUS [S13]). BDL publishes it only nationally from 2002; there is no urban/rural or place-class fertility in the primary sources used here.
- **Life expectancy at birth** rose for men from 69.7 (2000) to 75.4 (2025) and for women from 78.0 to 82.5; it fell to 71.8 and 79.7 in 2021 [S14]. The urban-rural gap in 2025 is 0.8 years for men (75.7 vs 74.9) and 0.1 for women [S14]; in 2000 rural women lived longer than urban women (78.4 vs 77.8) [S14].

## 3. Migration
- **Internal migration by place class** (net balance of registered permanent-residence moves, summed over gminas) shows suburbanisation clearly [S15][S1]. Villages gained people in every year, between +4 thousand (2000) and +53 thousand (2022), i.e. +2.4 per 1000 in 2025; small cities lost in every year (-0.5 per 1000 in 2000, -3.4 in 2022, -2.6 in 2025); big cities gained slightly in 2000-2002 (+0.2 to +0.4 per 1000), lost people in 2003-2013 (worst -1.7 per 1000 in 2007), gained again in 2014-2019 (up to +0.8 in 2019) and lost again from 2020 (-1.2 in 2022, -0.4 in 2025) (derived, `migration_by_place_class.csv`). The three nets sum to zero each year. A city-to-city versus city-to-village origin-destination matrix is not published by BDL in aggregate and is not provided (see gaps). Interpretation in the literature is consistent with this: flows go from the core city into surrounding municipalities [S6][S7][S28].
- **International migration, GUS**: net registered international migration for permanent residence was -19.7 thousand in 2000 and -12.9 thousand in 2005, turned positive around 2016, and reached +10.9 thousand in 2025; registered departures abroad fell from 27.0 to 10.2 thousand and arrivals rose from 7.3 to 21.1 thousand [S15]. These count only changes of permanent residence registration. 2015 is missing in BDL (reported as 0) and is left empty here.
- **International migration, Eurostat**: immigration of 300 thousand and emigration of 206 thousand in 2024 [S22] are on a different registration basis, with a level break between 2008 (about 15 thousand) and 2009 (about 189 thousand in the unflagged series); they must not be compared with the GUS permanent-residence figures. Eurostat's "net migration plus statistical adjustment" (a population-balance residual) was +33.2 thousand in 2024 and +2.8 thousand in 2025 [S20].
- **Ukrainian inflow after February 2022, kept separate** (`ukraine_inflow.csv`): the stock of Ukrainian citizens under EU temporary protection in Poland rose from 0.68 million (March 2022) to a peak of 1.37 million (September 2022), settled near 0.95-0.99 million through 2023-2025 (0.96 million in December 2022, 0.99 million in December 2024, 0.97 million in December 2025), and was 0.86 million in September 2026, the latest month [S23]. This is a stock of status holders, not net migration, and it is not part of any GUS series above. Literature describes the 2022 wave as the largest since World War II and notes refugees settle mainly near large cities [S27].

## 4. Households
- **Census counts**: 13.34 million households with 2.84 persons on average in 2002 (derived from [S16]), 13.57 million and 2.82 in 2011 [S17], and 12.52 million and 2.99 in 2021 [S18]. One-person households were 24.8% in 2002 and 24.0% in 2011, and 22.6% in 2021 (derived).
- The 2021 census figures (fewer households, bigger average size, fewer one-person households than in 2011) run against the long-term fall of household size shown by the survey series below; BDL gives no concept note, so the three census points may not be strictly comparable and no trend should be drawn from them without a GUS methodological note (not found in the time available).
- **Annual estimate**: the EU Labour Force Survey average falls from 3.0 persons per private household in 2006 to 2.5 in 2021 and 2.3 in 2025 [S24]. It is published rounded to one decimal, is survey-based, and differs from the 2021 census level (2.5 vs 2.99) [S18][S24].
- No household data by place class: the gmina-level 2021 table was not fetched (time-boxed, see gaps).

## 5. Suburban belt versus shrinking small towns
- **Belt method**: all gminas whose centre is within 25 km of one of the 8 largest cities (Warsaw, Kraków, Wrocław, Łódź, Poznań, Gdańsk, Szczecin, Lublin), assigned to the nearest, excluding the 34 big cities themselves [S1][S26]. 132 belt gminas have both 2000 and 2025 values; together they grew from 2.14 to 3.01 million (+41%) [S1].
- **Belt versus core, 2000 to 2025** (derived, `suburban_belt_summary.csv`): Warsaw core +11.6%, belt +47.5%; Kraków +7.6% vs +32.3%; Wrocław +5.0% vs +102.1% (10 gminas); Poznań -8.3% vs +79.4%; Gdańsk +5.7% vs +55.0%; Szczecin -7.9% vs +29.0%; Łódź -19.9% vs +6.6%; Lublin -9.0% vs +11.5% [S1]. In Łódź, Szczecin and Lublin the belt includes several shrinking industrial towns, so the belt average understates the pure suburban villages.
- **Fastest-growing gminas** (population 2000 to 2025): Dobra (Szczecińska) 8.6 to 31.6 thousand (+266%), Dopiewo (+249%) and Rokietnica (+229%) and Komorniki (+221%) near Poznań, Czernica near Wrocław (+220%), Lesznowola near Warsaw (+220%), then Kleszczewo, Pruszcz Gdański, Kórnik and Jabłonna (+151% to +165%) [S1]. Of the 132 belt gminas, 20 lost population, among them Sopot (-26%), Pabianice (-19%), Lubartów (-16%), Nałęczów (-15%) and Zgierz (-11%) [S1].
- **Small towns** (urban gminas and town parts of urban-rural gminas below 100,000 inhabitants in 2025, with at least 2,000 inhabitants in 2000): of 777 such towns, 649 lost population and 127 gained; together they went from 12.78 to 11.59 million (-9.4%) [S1]. The largest relative losses are Hel (-39.7%, 4.4 to 2.7 thousand), Zawadzkie (-31.0%), Dęblin (-29.7%), Duszniki-Zdrój (-28.9%), Zdzieszowice (-28.7%) and Reszel (-28.6%); the biggest gainer among them is Siechnice near Wrocław (+192%) [S1]. The cause of individual losses (for example military or boundary effects at Hel) was not checked.

## Data gaps and breaks
- **Place-class level missing**: age and sex pyramids, median age before 2016, dependency ratios before 2012 (urban/rural), fertility, migration origin-destination (city to city / city to village) and household size outside 2021 are not available by place class from the primary sources used; the 2021 household table at gmina level and net migration abroad at gmina level were not fetched (about 42 requests each under the anonymous BDL limit; add the variable ids to `scripts/fetch_bdl_gminas.py` to include them).
- **Series starts**: GUS TFR from 2002 (2000-2001 from Eurostat), dependency ratios from 2002/2003, median age from 2016/2018, Eurostat Ukrainian-citizen immigration has only 2 observations and is unused.
- **Breaks**: census rebasing in 2002, 2011 and 2021 (population 2020+ recalculated), the 2009/2010 population-concept change (see README), the Eurostat immigration/emigration level break 2008/2009, an apparent drop of registered departures abroad between 2014 (28,080) and 2016 (11,970) not explained in BDL, BDL zeros for 2015 international migration (treated as missing), and COVID-19 mortality in 2020-2021.
- **Reference dates** differ: BDL 31 December, Eurostat 1 January (pyramid and ratios), calendar-year flows for births, deaths and migration.
- **Territory**: gminas created, merged or re-coded after 2000 have no continuous series (flagged in the `note` column); gmina type changes are joined by 6-digit TERYT; town-rights changes move people between villages and small cities and were not corrected.
- **Belt definition** (25 km, centroid of simplified polygons, nearest of 8 cities) is a judgement call; results near the radius edge are sensitive to it.
- **Households**: 2002/2011/2021 counts may not be comparable (see section 4); EU-LFS is rounded and survey-based.
- **Not checked**: the GUS publication "Demographic situation in Poland up to 2024" [S25] was not read; no figure comes from it.
