# 03 - Poland in Europe: Eurostat comparison, 2000-latest

Comparison set: Poland (PL), Czechia (CZ), Slovakia (SK), Hungary (HU), Germany (DE), Romania (RO), Lithuania (LT) and the EU-27 aggregate (`EU27_2020`). All numbers come from Eurostat (one dataset per indicator, code in `data/processed/europe_*.csv`); "rank" means Poland's position among the seven countries (1 = highest value), derived in `europe_summary.csv`. Eurostat quality flags (b = break in series, e = estimated, p = provisional) are kept in the data; many series have breaks, so single-year jumps need a look at the flag.

**Latest year per indicator:** population on 1 January 2026; population change, natural change and net migration 2025; median age and old-age dependency 1 January 2025; total fertility rate and life expectancy 2024; degree-of-urbanisation shares 2025 (survey) and 2021 (census). Latest values for 2024-2026 are largely provisional (flag p).

## 1. Where Poland stands out
| indicator | Poland | EU-27 | Poland's rank of 7 | source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Population 1 Jan 2026 (1 Jan 2000 = 100) | 94.95 | 105.49 | fourth of the four countries that lost population (LT 82.2, RO 84.8, HU 92.8 lost more) | [S29] derived |
| Total fertility rate 2024 | 1.14 (1.37 in 2000) | 1.34 | 6 (only LT, 1.11, lower) | [S30] |
| Median age 1 Jan 2025 | 43.4 (35.1 in 2000) | 44.9 | 6 (only SK, 43.0, younger) | [S31] |
| Old-age dependency 2025 (65+ per 100 aged 15-64) | 32.7 (17.8 in 2000) | 34.5 | 2 (DE 35.9 higher) | [S31] |
| Life expectancy at birth 2024, both sexes | 78.5 (73.8 in 2000) | 81.5 | 3 | [S32] |
| Net migration plus adjustment 2025, per 1000 | 0.1 | 4.6 | 6 (tied with HU 0.1) | [S29] |

- **Ageing speed is the clearest outlier.** Old-age dependency rose by 14.9 points between 2000 and 2025 (+83.7%), the largest rise of the seven countries; the next are CZ +12.6, RO +12.3, DE +12.0, LT +11.3, SK +12.1, HU +10.2 and EU-27 +11.1 (2001-2025) [S31] (derived, `europe_summary.csv`). Poland started well below the EU level (18.0 vs 23.4 in 2001) and ended close to it (32.7 vs 34.5) [S31]. Median age rose 8.3 years (35.1 to 43.4), less than RO (+9.7) and SK (+9.1) and about the same as LT (+8.5), but more than CZ (+7.0), HU (+6.3), DE (+5.7) and the EU-27 (+6.5 since 2001) [S31]. Poland is still younger than the EU-27 (43.4 vs 44.9) but the gap to the EU-27 has narrowed from 3.0 years in 2001 (35.4 vs 38.4) to 1.5 in 2025 [S31] (derived).
- **Fertility fell the opposite way to most neighbours.** Between 2000 and 2024 Poland's TFR fell from 1.37 to 1.14 (-0.23), while CZ rose from 1.15 to 1.36, SK from 1.30 to 1.46, HU from 1.32 to 1.41 and RO from 1.31 to 1.39; only LT fell more (1.39 to 1.11) [S30]. In 2000 Poland (1.37) was above CZ, SK, HU and RO; in 2024 it is below all of them and below DE (1.36) too [S30]. Poland peaked at 1.48 in 2017 (HU 1.56, CZ 1.69 that year) [S30]. The GUS figure for 2025 (1.07, in task 02) is not in this comparison because Eurostat's latest year is 2024.
- **Population: a loss while the EU grew.** Poland's population on 1 January fell from 38.26 million (2000) to 36.33 million (2026), -5.05%, while the EU-27 grew 5.49% and CZ 6.2%, DE 1.6% [S29] (derived). Poland's decline began in earnest later than Romania's and Lithuania's: index 99.4 in 2010 (RO 90.4, LT 89.5) and 99.2 in 2020 (RO 86.1, LT 80.0), then 94.95 in 2026 [S29]. The steep fall happens in 2021-2026: from 37.07 million on 1 January 2021 to 36.33 million on 1 January 2026 [S29]. Natural change was -167,483 in 2025 against +10,321 in 2000 [S29]; CZ had a small deficit already in 2000 (-18,091) [S29].
- **Eurostat versus GUS population level.** Eurostat's 1 January 2026 figure (36,332,765) is about 1.0 million below GUS's 31 December 2025 figure (37,332,510, task 01) [S29][S1]. The reason (population concept, treatment of long-term absentees, revision timing) was not verified; the two series are not mixed anywhere in this project.
- **Migration: Poland is not a destination in the registers.** Net migration plus statistical adjustment was +0.1 per 1000 in 2025 (2,753 persons) against +4.6 in the EU-27, +3.9 in CZ, +5.4 in RO, +5.8 in LT and +2.9 in DE [S29]. The peak was +0.9 per 1000 (33,247 persons) in 2024 [S29]. In 2020 the series shows -762,735 persons (-20.3 per 1000), against a total population change of -884,781 and natural change of -122,046 in the same table [S29]. This is mostly a statistical adjustment (flag b), not emigration; its cause is not stated in the dataset and the 2020 value should be excluded from any migration trend. The huge 2022 values of CZ (+30.9 per 1000) and LT (+25.5) are the Ukrainian war inflow; Poland's own 2022 value is only +0.2 [S29]: the Ukrainian inflow does not show up in this Eurostat series (the stock of temporary-protection holders is in task 02 [S23]); why was not investigated. The label "net migration" here is a residual, not a count of moves.
- **Life expectancy: catching up, not caught up.** Poland gained 4.7 years (73.8 to 78.5) between 2000 and 2024 against 3.9 for the EU-27 (2002-2024) and 2.9 for Germany [S32]; the gap to the EU-27 is still 3.0 years (78.5 vs 81.5) [S32]. Poland is third of seven, above SK (78.4), LT (77.5), HU (76.8) and RO (76.5), below CZ (80.1) and DE (81.2) [S32]. Men: 74.8 (rank 4), women: 82.2 (rank 3) [S32]. The 2020-2021 drop is visible (76.4 in 2020, 75.4 in 2021, vs 78.5 in 2024) [S32].

## 2. Urban and rural: Eurostat's degree of urbanisation (DEGURBA)
Eurostat does not publish a Poland-by-city-size comparison. The only harmonised split is the **degree of urbanisation**: cities (DEGURBA 1), towns and suburbs (2), rural areas (3) [S36]. Three sources are stored in `europe_degurba.csv`; they differ in nature and must not be combined:

| measure | dataset | nature | years for Poland |
|---|---|---|---|
| share of all persons | ilc_lvho01 [S33] | EU-SILC survey | 2005-2025 |
| population aged 15+, thousand | lfsa_pgauws [S34] | EU-LFS survey | 2006-2025 |
| population count | cens_21urb01 [S35] | 2021 census | 2021 only |

**Census 2021 benchmark** (the most reliable level, an actual count): Poland had 12,453,650 people in cities (33.64%), 11,731,735 in towns and suburbs (31.69%) and 12,834,067 in rural areas (34.67%) [S35] (shares derived). Peers: cities / towns and suburbs / rural areas as shares were CZ 28.5 / 37.0 / 34.5, SK 21.0 / 38.0 / 40.9, HU 33.2 / 36.8 / 30.0, DE 36.8 / 43.4 / 19.8, RO 31.3 / 27.5 / 41.1, LT 44.5 / 14.7 / 40.9, EU-27 39.1 / 36.4 / 24.5 [S35]. Poland's rural share (34.67%) is 10.2 points above the EU-27 (24.48%) and in the same range as CZ and SK; its city share (33.64%) is 5.5 points below the EU-27 [S35] (derived).

**Dynamics from the surveys** (Poland, 2012 to 2025, ilc_lvho01, after the break between 2011 and 2012; the 2012 value is flagged b [S33]): the share in cities went from 33.9% to 34.3% (+0.4 points), in towns and suburbs from 24.5% to 30.3% (+5.8) and in rural areas from 41.6% to 35.4% (-6.2) [S33] (derived). The EU-27 moved similarly: cities 39.3% to 38.5%, towns and suburbs 29.6% to 37.0% (+7.4), rural 31.0% to 24.5% (-6.5) [S33] (EU-27 values 2010-2018 flagged e = estimated). The labour-force survey gives the same direction for people aged 15+: Poland's rural share 40.3% (2012) to 35.9% (2025), towns and suburbs 23.8% to 27.7%, cities 36.0% to 36.5% [S34] (derived). So the survey picture is **cities stable, towns and suburbs growing, rural shrinking** - a suburbanisation signature - in Poland as in the whole EU. The survey series cannot separate real movement from reclassification when Eurostat updates the grid (the 2011 to 2012 jump in Poland is -5.9 points for cities and +9.3 for towns and suburbs [S33] (derived), far larger than any real shift in one year), so the size of the shift is uncertain; the direction agrees in two surveys.

**The rural share rose in GUS data but fell in DEGURBA.** GUS's rural share of the population (legal definition) rose from 39.44% in 2012 to 40.66% in 2025 [S1] (derived, task 01) while the DEGURBA rural share fell by about 6 points in the surveys. The two are measuring different things (section 3). A likely explanation (not tested at gmina level): villages kept gaining people through suburban migration (task 02) while legal status (town rights) changes slowly, whereas DEGURBA follows built-up density, so fast-growing suburban gminas can move into "towns and suburbs".

## 3. Definitions: GUS vs. Eurostat (what is a city, a rural area)
| | GUS (as used in tasks 01 and 02) | Eurostat DEGURBA |
|---|---|---|
| basis | legal / administrative status | density of 1 km2 grid cells |
| city / urban | a settlement with town rights; in TERYT, urban gminas (type 1) and towns in urban-rural gminas (type 4) [S38]; urban population = people living in localities with city status [S40] | **cities**: at least 50% of the population of the local unit (gmina in Poland) lives in urban centres (contiguous cells of at least 1,500 inhabitants per km2 and at least 50,000 inhabitants) [S36][S37] |
| middle class | none (a town of 3,000 and a city of 1.8 million are both "urban") | **towns and suburbs**: less than 50% in urban centres but at least 50% in urban clusters (at least 300 inhabitants per km2, at least 5,000 inhabitants) [S37] |
| rural | the rural gminas and the non-town part of urban-rural gminas (types 2 and 5) [S39] | **rural areas**: more than 50% of the population in rural grid cells (below the cluster thresholds) [S37] |
| unit | gmina, with a town part and a rural part in urban-rural gminas | the whole local administrative unit (gmina) gets one class; the classification is not split inside a gmina |
| changes | by decision (new town rights, boundary changes); rebased at censuses | recalculated from the population grid when Eurostat updates it (method fixed in 2011, grid from census rounds; 1991 version used LAU density) [S37] |

Consequences for Poland, all visible in the data above:
- A **small town** (GUS urban) can be "rural" in DEGURBA if it has too few people in contiguous dense cells, and a **large village or commuter gmina** with at least 5,000 inhabitants in dense cells counts as "towns and suburbs", although GUS calls it rural. Because the unit is the whole gmina, the urban part and rural part of an urban-rural gmina get the same class.
- In 2021, GUS counted 59.68% of the population as urban and 40.32% as rural [S1] (derived, task 01); DEGURBA (census) gives 65.33% in cities plus towns and suburbs and 34.67% in rural areas [S35] (derived). The GUS "big cities" class of this project (34 cities with at least 100,000 inhabitants, 27.03% in 2021 [S1]) is a different concept from DEGURBA "cities" (33.64% [S35]); the two must not be compared as if they were the same class. The 5.7-point rural gap (40.32% vs 34.67%) is a definition effect on the same census year, not a data error; that the density classification classes more people as non-rural is consistent with the suburbanisation described in task 02, but the gmina-level class assignment was not obtained, so which gminas move was not checked.
- Eurostat's classification applies to every EU country with the same rule, so the peer comparison is like for like; GUS-style legal status is not, because countries define "town" very differently (this was not checked country by country).

## 4. Caveats and what is not here
- **UN World Population Prospects and OECD were not used.** Every indicator requested is available for all eight areas in Eurostat for 2000 onward, and Eurostat is primary for these countries. UN WPP would add modelled values that differ in method (the World Bank/UN series for Poland already differ from GUS by about 0.9 million in 2025, README); mixing them would not add comparability. The WPP and OECD portals were not queried.
- **Series breaks.** Poland's Eurostat series carry b flags in 2000, 2010 and 2021 (census rebasing); EU-27 population and migration have b flags nearly every year because the aggregate sums countries with different revisions [S29]. EU-27 starts in 2001 for fertility, median age and dependency, and in 2002 for life expectancy [S30][S31][S32].
- **Net migration is a residual** (balance of the population change not explained by births and deaths), with large level breaks (Poland 2020).
- **DEGURBA survey series** are not usable across the 2011-2012 classification change; the 2021 census is the only count. Gaps are left empty: EU-27 starts in 2010 (ilc_lvho01); LFS urbanisation split starts 2006 for Poland.
- **No place-class (big city / small city / village) comparison** is possible for other countries: Eurostat has no population-by-city-size tables for the comparison set at annual frequency in the datasets used; DEGURBA is the best available level, stated as such.
